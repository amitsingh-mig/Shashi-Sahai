Shashi Sahai: The Man Who Saw Stories Others Missed

New Delhi · 1960 – 2026

Veteran journalist, photographer and philanthropist Shashi Sahai, whose camera documented countless lives while he quietly devoted his own to photography, friendship and social service, passed away in New Delhi on August 15 after a prolonged battle with cancer. He was 66.

For much of his life, Shashi stood behind the lens. It was where he was most comfortable — watching, listening and waiting for the moment that others often missed.

A Photojournalist by profession, a photographer by instinct, he had a long association with the media and contributed to several publications, including international magazines. Colleagues remember him as a keen observer with a rare ability to find a story in the seemingly ordinary.

His photographs reflected the way he saw the world. He was interested not merely in faces, but in the lives behind them. A passing expression, a conversation on a street, a child at play, an animal, an unnoticed corner of everyday life — Sahai could find meaning in moments most people would simply walk past. For him, photography was less about the camera and more about paying attention.

Ironically, the man who spent decades documenting others rarely wanted attention himself.

Shashi remained intensely private and understated throughout his life. He was uncomfortable with self-promotion and rarely spoke about his own achievements. Those close to him say he would much rather hear another person's story than tell his own.

As cofounder and a long-standing pillar of Aarohan NGO, located at Malviya Nagar, New Delhi, he worked for years in support of children, women and vulnerable communities. His contribution went far beyond designation. He became one of the people the organization could depend upon — someone who worked quietly, solved problems and remained present when he was needed.

He did not believe every act of kindness required an audience. Much of what he did for others was done without photographs, announcements or recognition.

Perhaps that was the clearest reflection of the man he was. He could meet a stranger and, before long, make that person feel like an old friend — irrespective of age, profession or social standing. People entered his life as acquaintances and often stayed as family.

But the final months of Sahai's life were very different from the years he had spent moving through the world with a camera in hand. Yet cancer is only the final paragraph of his story. It is not the story itself.

That story lies in the decades that came before it. It is in the photographs he left behind and the stories he helped tell. It is in Aarohan and the communities he served. It is in the people he helped when nobody was watching. It is in friendships that outlived assignments, workplaces and distances.

In the memories of those who knew a very different Shashi Sahai from the name that appeared alongside published work.

He was the person who called. The person who showed up. The person who remembered…

A Bond Beyond Definitions

Shashi Sahai: A Bond Beyond Definitions

Some relationships enter our lives without announcement and, over time, become so deeply woven into our existence that we no longer know where one person's world ends and the other's begins. My association with Shashi Sahai was one such relationship.

For nearly twenty years, we lived and worked alongside each other. We shared a commitment to causes larger than ourselves. Through Aarohan and beyond it, Shashi gave his time, energy, experience and affection without keeping an account of what he gave or what he received. There were no fixed hours for him when somebody needed help. Resources could be scarce, circumstances could be difficult, but these things rarely altered the purpose we shared.

We could disagree, but our differences never became distance. There was an extraordinary freedom in our relationship. Neither time, money, position nor circumstance could determine its strength.

Shashi had a quality that is increasingly rare: he knew how to make another human being feel important. He gave affection without measuring status, age, gender or social position. Children responded to him naturally. Friends relied upon him. People carrying difficulties often found themselves speaking to him because he could listen without making their vulnerability smaller.

"For me, his support was something I once described as an afternoon shadow—sometimes you may not notice it, but it remains beside you."

Whether I was dealing with a difficult decision, an organisational crisis, a personal worry or simply the exhaustion of the day, I knew Shashi was there. Sometimes that assurance required no conversation at all.

He was also a puzzle. Even those who knew him for years probably knew only parts of him. He seldom spoke about his own agony. He could carry hurt quietly and still find the emotional space to comfort somebody else. Perhaps that was one of his contradictions: a man who concealed his own wounds but was extraordinarily sensitive to the wounds of others.

Our relationship never needed a name. At different moments he could be my closest friend, my critic, my guide, my mentor, my companion in a cause, and the person before whom I could put down the burdens I carried. We did not spend our years defining the relationship; we spent them living it.

His final illness changed the rhythm of our lives. When cancer gradually took away his strength and independence, our family cared for him through treatment, hospitalisations and finally at home. Watching someone who had given so much of himself become physically vulnerable was profoundly painful.

Shashi left us on 15 August 2026. Yet even now, I find it difficult to write that he is “gone.” There are moments when I instinctively want to tell him something. There are decisions during which I can almost anticipate what he would have said. There are corners of the house, memories, photographs, children and years of Aarohan's journey in which his presence continues.

Today, I do not feel that I carry only memories of Shashi. I carry something of Shashi himself within me. And perhaps somewhere in everything that we built, everyone he loved, and every life he touched, he continues to live.

Rani Patel Founder & President, Aarohan NGO

Patience, Resilience & Innovation

My professional journey with Shashi Sir taught me what patience, resilience and innovation truly mean. He had an extraordinary ability to look beyond our first fears and see possibilities in people and places others might overlook.

When Aarohan first received an opportunity to work with the transgender community, I was apprehensive and even suggested that we return the project. Shashi Sir simply said, “Unless we understand their lives in depth, how can we decide whether we can work with them?” That thought changed our direction and eventually became one of Aarohan’s most meaningful journeys.

I saw the same spirit at Jamunwala Park, where working with highly vulnerable children initially seemed almost impossible. Through photography, storytelling, patience and, above all, relationships with the children and their parents, he transformed hostility into trust. Children who once threw stones at the Pustak Mahal began coming there to read, take photographs, play their dhol and cheer for Shashi Sir.

And in the remote tribal areas of Araku, he spent nearly six months on the ground, understanding communities and exploring practical ways to support them.

"These experiences define Shashi Sir professionally — a man of immense patience and resilience, always searching for an innovative way forward and always willing to stand beside people whom society had overlooked."

When I see the transformation that grew from those beginnings, I feel deeply proud that our professional journeys travelled together for so many years. His work will continue to speak through the lives that changed.

Master of the Lens & Cannes Winner

Shashi Sahai belonged to a rare breed of photojournalists whose images resonated equally in international design forums and quiet village squares. Receiving the prestigious Bronze at the Cannes Lions International Festival of Creativity marked national recognition for his mastery over light, composition, and human emotion.

Over three decades, his editorial work graced major publications across Asia, Europe, and the Americas. Yet Shashi Sir remained indifferent to personal glamour. To him, the camera was never a barrier—it was an instrument of empathy.

"His frames did not exploit vulnerability; they elevated human dignity. Shashi Sahai gave world-class artistry to grassroots human stories."

From darkroom silver-gelatin prints in 1991 to digital visual archives, his legacy remains an indispensable resource for documentary photojournalism.

Empowering Youth & Grassroots Communities

Beyond his cameras and archives, Shashi Sahai was the heartbeat of Aarohan NGO's community outreach. He believed that every child, regardless of social status, possessed an innate story worth capturing and telling.

He conducted photography and creative storytelling workshops for underprivileged children, giving them cameras to view their own neighborhoods through lenses of hope and pride. In Araku Valley, he helped establish local livelihood initiatives that continue to sustain tribal families today.

"Shashi Sir did not just teach children how to take photos; he taught us to see beauty, purpose, and confidence within ourselves."

His kindness created ripples that will endure through generations of youth whose lives he touched directly.
