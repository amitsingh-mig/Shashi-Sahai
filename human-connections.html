The space between people.

An offered bite, a direct gaze and a face behind ironwork reveal three different ways a photograph brings us near.

Closeness is not always a matter of distance. It can be held in a hand, in the direction of a gaze, or in the small interval between a person and a window. These portraits offer different kinds of encounter, leaving room for the people in them to remain more than a description.

01 / A CLOSER LOOK

A gesture before a story

A hand offers food to a young girl in a pink sweater. That small movement links the two figures more immediately than anything in the background. The glowing lights provide colour and atmosphere, but the exchange takes place within a much smaller circle: hand, mouth, face, glance. We can recognise the action without supplying a relationship or inventing a conversation. The photograph gives us enough to look closely, and no reason to pretend we know everything. Its intimacy comes from an observable gesture shared within a world that continues beyond the frame.

02 / A CLOSER LOOK

Looking back

Beside the green rickshaw, the man in the yellow vest looks toward the camera. The vehicle occupies a substantial part of the frame, giving the portrait a setting rather than an empty backdrop. Green and yellow bring the two main forms into clear relation; the street behind them adds another layer of people and movement. Start with the face, then look outward to the edges. The portrait changes as the surrounding details return. A direct gaze can feel like a meeting, even while the photograph preserves the distance between a viewer and a person whose full story is unknown.

03 / A CLOSER LOOK

A view through a pattern

Repeating iron arches divide the window into smaller views. The older man wearing glasses is close to this curved grille, his face turned toward the light beyond it. The pattern is immediately noticeable, yet it does not replace the person: each return to the face changes how the surrounding lines feel. Follow one curve, then another, and notice where the eye finally rests. The photograph holds a human profile and an architectural pattern together. We can describe that relationship precisely without deciding whether the man is waiting, remembering, or simply watching what is outside.
